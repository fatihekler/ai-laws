## 3. European Union baseline

### TFEU Article 101

The current official EUR-Lex source confirms Article 101 as the Union rule addressing agreements between undertakings, decisions by associations of undertakings and concerted practices that may affect Member-State trade and have the object or effect of preventing, restricting or distorting competition. R016 therefore requires evidence of a legally relevant agreement/decision/concerted practice before moving from parallel statements or public safety alignment to an Article 101 theory.

Safety purpose does not create an antitrust exemption. Conversely, safety-oriented cooperation is not automatically restrictive. The actual restraint, information exchanged, necessity, proportionality, access conditions, market coverage, governance and competitive effects remain material.

### 2023 Horizontal Cooperation Guidelines

The Commission's 2023 horizontal guidelines are `OFFICIAL_GUIDANCE`, not treaty text or a court holding. They provide a structured Article 101 framework for R&D, purchasing, commercialisation, information exchange, standardisation and related cooperation.

For AI safety coordination, R016 extracts a design-oriented competition screen rather than a safe harbor:

- limit competitively sensitive information exchange to what is objectively necessary for the legitimate cooperation;
- avoid using standards to exclude rivals or coordinate dimensions of competition beyond the technical/safety objective;
- favor transparent procedures and meaningful participation where the standard materially structures market access;
- avoid compulsory compliance unless legally required or otherwise justified under the applicable competition framework;
- preserve effective and non-discriminatory access to standards or essential outputs where foreclosure risk is material;
- assess whether the standard reaches farther than necessary to achieve safety, quality, compatibility or interoperability benefits.

These are guidance-derived analytical considerations; they are not a universal formula proving legality.

### TFEU Article 102

Article 102 addresses abuse by one or more dominant undertakings. R016 does not equate firm size, an oligopolistic market, technical leadership, lobbying activity or high compute ownership with abuse. Market definition, dominance and legally relevant abusive conduct remain separate elements.

Potential AI-relevant theories may involve exclusionary access terms, tying/bundling, discriminatory access to key inputs or other conduct, but no such theory is admitted as a violation without actor- and market-specific evidence.

### EU Merger Regulation

Council Regulation (EC) No 139/2004 supplies the EU concentration-control framework and the significant-impediment-to-effective-competition test. AI investments or cloud/model partnerships are not automatically concentrations. Control, transaction structure, jurisdiction and competitive effect must be established separately.

The Regulation itself recognizes that oligopolistic markets can remain competitively healthy; concentration analysis therefore cannot be replaced by a simple count of large firms.

### Digital Markets Act

Regulation (EU) 2022/1925 is a binding ex-ante digital-markets regime for designated gatekeepers and covered core platform services. Its contestability/fairness architecture and access/interoperability/data-related duties can be relevant to lock-in and entry analysis where the statutory scope is actually met. It is not a general AI-company competition statute and does not replace Articles 101/102 or merger control.

### Commission Generative-AI competition policy brief

The Commission Competition Policy Brief on generative AI and virtual worlds discusses access to inputs such as compute/data/talent, partnerships and market-development risks. R016 classifies it as `OFFICIAL_STAFF_ANALYSIS`; concern identification is not a violation finding.

## 4. Cross-authority AI competition statement

The joint EC/CMA/DOJ/FTC statement on competition in generative-AI foundation models and AI products identifies common competition principles and risks while preserving each authority's sovereign decision-making. It is an enforcement-policy signal, not a new multinational antitrust statute and not proof of misconduct by any firm.

## 5. United States federal baseline

### Sherman Act § 1 — 15 U.S.C. § 1

The current Office of Law Revision Counsel source was acquired and marker-verified. Section 1 addresses contract, combination or conspiracy in restraint of trade. R016 therefore keeps the agreement element separate from market parallelism and public alignment.

```text
SIMILAR_PUBLIC_SAFETY_POSITION
!= AGREEMENT_PROVEN
```

A competition analysis of AI safety cooperation must identify who agreed to what, the restraint's relationship to a legitimate joint activity, the information exchanged, market effects and the applicable mode of antitrust analysis.

### Sherman Act § 2 — 15 U.S.C. § 2

Section 2 supplies the monopolization/attempt/conspiracy-to-monopolize statutory layer. Market power and exclusionary conduct cannot be inferred merely from high market share, model capability, compute ownership, political visibility or oligopolistic structure.

### Clayton Act § 7 — 15 U.S.C. § 18

Section 7 addresses acquisitions whose effect may be substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly. The 2023 Merger Guidelines are current nonbinding enforcement guidance and expressly create no independent rights or obligations.

For AI partnerships and investments, the Guidelines support attention to concentration trends, coordination risk, access to important inputs, vertical/platform relationships and partial/minority ownership. Those considerations do not establish that a particular partnership is a merger, is reportable, or is unlawful.

### FTC Act § 5 — 15 U.S.C. § 45

The current statutory source prohibits unfair methods of competition. R016 does not convert generic concerns about AI market structure, switching costs, standards, lobbying or partnership design into a Section 5 violation without a legally supported conduct theory and facts.

## 6. Safety-information-sharing boundary

The 2014 DOJ/FTC Antitrust Policy Statement on Sharing of Cybersecurity Information remains listed in current DOJ guidance and was body/marker-verified. The agencies expressly preserved that statement when withdrawing the 2000 competitor-collaboration guidelines.

R016 uses the document only as a narrow analogy: properly designed sharing of highly technical cyber-threat information can be compatible with antitrust law, especially where it is distinct from sharing competitively sensitive current/future price, output or strategic business information.

```text
CYBERSECURITY_INFORMATION_SHARING_GUIDANCE
!= AI_SAFETY_COLLABORATION_SAFE_HARBOR
```

Any AI-safety collaboration still requires its own legal and factual analysis.

## 7. Withdrawn collaboration guidance and 2026 policy process

DOJ/FTC withdrew the April 2000 Antitrust Guidelines for Collaborations Among Competitors in December 2024. The withdrawal statement specifically warned that the old guidelines no longer reliably addressed modern collaboration and technologies including artificial intelligence and algorithmic pricing.

In February 2026 the agencies opened a public inquiry seeking input for new business-collaboration guidance, and in April 2026 extended the comment deadline to May 21, 2026. R016 found no controlled source establishing a final replacement collaboration guideline as of 2026-09-14.

```text

2026_REQUEST_FOR_COMMENT != FINAL_GUIDANCE
```

## 8. FTC AI partnership study

The FTC's 6(b) staff report on cloud-service-provider / AI-developer partnerships was acquired as a 43-page official PDF and marker-verified. It identifies areas to watch including access to important inputs, switching or contractual/technical dependencies and access to sensitive technical/business information.

The report is a staff study, not an adjudication. Its observations may generate research questions, but they do not establish that the studied partnerships violated Sherman Act §1/§2, Clayton Act §7 or FTC Act §5.

## 9. Safety-standard design screen

For a future AI-safety coordination arrangement, R016 recommends preserving the following competition-law research facts before reaching any conclusion:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who are the actual/potential competitors? | defines horizontal relationship |
| What exact commitment exists? | distinguishes public alignment from agreement |
| Is compliance voluntary or compelled by law? | affects restraint characterization |
| What information is exchanged? | separates technical safety data from competitively sensitive strategy |
| Is the exchange objectively necessary and proportionate? | tests ancillary/efficiency logic |
| Are participation and rulemaking transparent? | tests exclusion/foreclosure risk |
| Can non-members effectively access the standard/output? | tests entry and interoperability effects |
| Are terms objective and non-discriminatory? | tests discriminatory foreclosure risk |
| Does governance allow incumbents to block rivals? | tests standard-setting capture/exclusion hypothesis |
| Does the arrangement regulate price, output, customers, wages, investment or innovation rivalry? | identifies core competitive dimensions |
| Are claimed safety benefits substantiated and linked to the restraint? | prevents purpose from substituting for analysis |
| Is there a less restrictive practical design? | informs necessity/proportionality assessment |

This table is an analytical research screen, not legal advice or a categorical safe harbor.

## 10. Market concentration, entry and regulatory capture

R016 distinguishes four questions:

1. **Concentration:** empirical market structure and transaction trends.
2. **Market power / dominance:** legally relevant ability under the applicable framework.
3. **Exclusion or coordination:** conduct and competitive effect.
4. **Regulatory capture:** evidence that regulation or institutional action has been improperly controlled or distorted by regulated interests.

High compliance costs, compute scarcity, access to data/talent, technical standards, cloud dependence and interoperability limits can all be investigated as entry-barrier hypotheses. None is automatically an antitrust violation.

Participation by a large AI firm in policy or standards development is not itself proof of regulatory capture. R016 admits no capture finding without actor-specific evidence of influence plus legally/materially relevant institutional distortion.
